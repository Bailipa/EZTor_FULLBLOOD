### 长篇匹配

Making a campus service work

A) A campus improvement project can look successful in photographs while making little difference to daily life. A neatly painted room or a new online form is easy to display. Whether students can actually use a service more easily is harder to show. The fictional project described here began when a university asked a student team to improve a small tools-lending service. Instead of immediately buying new tools, the team investigated how the existing service worked.

B) The first conversations produced several competing explanations. Some students said there were not enough tools. Others said they never knew whether the lending room was open. The team treated these comments as starting points rather than conclusions. It realised that people with different timetables could experience the same service very differently, even when they lived in neighbouring buildings.

C) For a week, the students observed the room before making any changes. They recorded how many people arrived, how many left without borrowing anything, and how long successful visits took. This provided a reference against which later changes could be judged. The team therefore included unsuccessful visits in its initial record, without collecting visitors' names.

D) One obstacle was the language on the cupboard labels. The labels used technical names that were familiar to experienced volunteers but confusing to many users. The team added photographs and short descriptions of what each tool could do. It kept the technical terms in smaller print, so experienced users could still find them.

E) Another difficulty was where tools was kept. Items needed for the same simple task were stored in different cupboards. Students repairing a loose bicycle part had to ask for several tools separately. The team prepared a small number of task-based kits and placed their contents on a checklist. Users could see what they needed before approaching the desk. Each item still had to be returned, but the search no longer began from nothing.

F) Changes to the room alone were insufficient. New volunteers were sometimes unsure how to answer questions and referred every visitor to the same experienced student. The team introduced a short practice session in which volunteers took turns acting as unfamiliar users. This exposed gaps in the instructions and gave volunteers experience explaining the service. The session focused on ordinary requests; it did not qualify anyone to advise on dangerous repairs or handle specialist electrical tools.

G) An online reservation form appeared to offer an easy solution to uncertain opening hours. However, the team kept a paper booking sheet as well. Some visitors arrived without a charged phone, while others found it easier to ask a question in person. Replacing every existing method with one digital route could create a new obstacle while removing an old one. The two methods were reviewed together to avoid promising the same kit to different users.

H) Not every trial worked. The team moved a return box close to the building entrance, expecting people to bring tools back more promptly. Returns did arrive earlier, but several kits came back with parts missing because no volunteer was present to check them. The team ended that trial and restored a staffed return point.

I) After two weeks, the team repeated its original observations at the same times of day. Comparing a busy Monday with a quiet weekend would have produced a misleading result. The students also checked whether a campus event had temporarily changed demand. Their record suggested shorter visits and fewer people leaving without tools, but they described this as a result of a small local trial, not proof that the arrangement would work everywhere.

J) The budget record included more than the price of new materials. Checklists had to be printed, kits had to be checked, and damaged items occasionally needed replacement. Volunteer time mattered too. A service that looked cheap because its organisers worked late every evening might not continue after they graduated. The team therefore estimated the regular work required each week and discussed which duties the university could realistically support in the longer term.

K) Safety established a firm boundary for the project. The lending service provided only tools suitable for basic tasks and displayed clear limits on its use. Items requiring specialist training remained outside the scheme, however often students requested them. The team understood that making a service more convenient did not justify ignoring risk. When a request fell outside the volunteers' knowledge, they explained the limit and suggested contacting a qualified repair service instead.

L) Before changing the opening hours, the students consulted the staff who managed the building. Users preferred evening access, but cleaning and security arrangements also had to be considered. The final schedule added two evening sessions rather than keeping the room open every night. This was less ambitious than the first proposal, yet it could be supported by the people responsible for the space. The team regarded that agreement as part of the design work itself.

M) Feedback did not end when the first changes were introduced. A small card beside the desk asked visitors to describe the most difficult part of their visit. It avoided the broad question, "Are you satisfied?", which often produced an answer too general to guide improvements. Cards were reviewed once a week, and a nearby notice explained which suggestions had been acted on. Comments containing personal information were not displayed publicly.

N) Other departments asked whether they could adopt the same arrangements. The team advised them to begin with a small trial and examine their own borrowing patterns. A laboratory with delicate instruments would have different requirements from a room lending bicycle pumps. The team shared its observation sheets and questions, but it did not present the exact cupboard layout as a universal solution. What could travel most easily was the process of investigating a need.

O) The project gave its organisers a less dramatic view of innovation. Improvement sometimes meant changing a label or arranging a dependable schedule, rather than inventing a new device. The students learned to judge a proposal by the obstacle it removed and the work needed to maintain it. A service remained useful when the next group of volunteers could understand it, run it safely, and discover what should be improved next.

### 仔细阅读 1

The boundary between quiet study and group discussion is not always clear. A fictional campus project explored whether a change in signs could help students choose an appropriate place without requiring staff to interrupt them repeatedly.
The library's signs simply named its quiet and discussion floors, assuming that the labels explained the expected behaviour. Interviews suggested otherwise. Some first-time visitors thought "discussion" meant that phone calls were allowed anywhere. Others took "quiet" to mean that they could not even ask a staff member a brief question. Experienced users had learned the rules through repeated visits, but newcomers had to guess.
The project team replaced the general labels with short descriptions of activities. One sign explained where whispered questions could be asked on the quiet floor. The team also put information at the entrance, before visitors had chosen a seat. A sign encountered only after someone had unpacked a bag could be accurate and still arrive too late to be helpful.
During the following fortnight, staff recorded fewer occasions on which they needed to explain the basic rules. The team did not assume that the signs alone had caused the change. The library was less busy in the second week, and some visitors had already seen the new information several times. Those factors could also influence behaviour. A later observation during a busier period would be needed before drawing a firmer conclusion.
The most useful lesson concerned the purpose of a sign. A label may describe a place from the manager's point of view while leaving a visitor uncertain about what to do. Information becomes more helpful when it appears at the moment of decision and answers the user's likely question. Adding more instructions is not necessarily the solution: a crowded notice can be as difficult to understand as a vague one. The team therefore removed repeated sentences instead of covering every wall with new signs. Its aim was to make expectations clear while preserving a welcoming place to study.

### 仔细阅读 2

Digital tools can make the early stages of a student project look unusually productive. Notes become neatly arranged, a plan appears in minutes, and an empty page quickly fills with paragraphs. Yet the ease of producing a visible result can make it harder to judge whether the underlying work has improved.
Consider a student preparing a short report on campus energy use. A tool may turn rough notes into a clear outline, but the student still needs to check whether the notes contain reliable measurements. If the original record mixes data from rooms of different sizes, a well-organised chart does not repair the comparison. The presentation may become clearer while the evidence remains weak.
The same distinction matters when students receive a polished paragraph. Reading it can create a sense of understanding because each sentence is easy to follow. Explaining the argument without looking at it is a different task. A student who cannot identify the relationship between a claim and its evidence may need to return to the source material rather than request another version of the paragraph.
This does not mean that students should avoid digital assistance. An outline can help them discover a missing step, and a suggested explanation can offer a starting point for revision. The important question is what students do next. One useful practice is to write down what they accepted, what they changed, and why. This makes their decisions visible and discourages the assumption that a fluent answer must also be an accurate one.
Teachers can ask students to explain one important choice alongside the finished work. There is no single amount of digital help that suits every task. Organising a timetable and evaluating evidence demand different kinds of judgment. A useful boundary is whether assistance leaves the student able to explain and defend the choices that matter. Where it does, a tool can save effort without removing responsibility. Where it does not, the speed of producing a result may hide a gap that the project was supposed to help the student address.

## 写作参考

Before proposing changes to a campus service, students should understand how people actually use it. A creative idea is valuable only when it addresses a real need.
First, observation can reveal obstacles that a quick discussion may miss. For example, students might assume that a long queue outside a service office requires another counter. However, watching the queue and asking users a few questions may show that unclear instructions cause repeated visits. A clear checklist could then be more useful than additional equipment.
Second, understanding users helps a project consider different situations. Students with morning classes and those with afternoon classes may need different opening hours. A small trial allows the team to compare results before making a permanent change.
In short, useful improvement begins with careful questions. By observing needs, testing an idea and listening to feedback, students can turn limited resources into a service that works better for more people.

播客制作与删减

For the next episode, the team chose one question about how a familiar household object worked. They kept only the information needed to answer it. A short example came before the technical term, and a brief summary appeared at the end. The episode was shorter, but a greater proportion of listeners reached the final minute.
The students now ask someone outside the team to listen to a draft. Rather than asking only whether it sounds interesting, they ask that person to explain the main idea afterwards. If the explanation is confused, they revise the structure before adding any more facts. They still check information carefully and provide sources in the programme notes. Reducing the amount of information has not meant reducing their responsibility for accuracy.
